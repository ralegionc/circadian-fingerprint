Actogram of the Internet

A sleep lab plots an animal's activity as hours across, days down, and reads the rhythm off the page. Do the same to two of the world's busiest websites and one of them turns out to keep a clock, the other a calendar.

Phase

Twenty-three editions, one shape

Each block below is four weeks of Wikipedia reading for one language edition: 24 columns for the hours, seven rows for the days, darker where more people were reading. Each block is scaled against its own average, so what you are comparing is shape, not size.

In UTC, the wall clock the servers keep, they look like twenty-three unrelated signals. Their busiest hours are scattered across twelve hours of the day. Switch to local time and they snap into alignment.

Entrainment

Everybody reads at nine in the evening

Pulling out just the busiest hour of each edition makes the collapse explicit. On the UTC clock the peaks are smeared across half a day. On the local clock, 21 of the 23 editions peak between 20:00 and 22:00.

Amplitude

How hard a platform breathes tells you where its readers live

Amplitude here is the ratio between an edition's busiest and quietest hour of the day. It looks like a measure of how devoted a readership is. It is really a measure of how tightly packed it is in longitude.

Dutch readers swing by a factor of 11.7 between their peak hour and their trough, because when it is 4am in the Netherlands it is 4am for almost everyone reading. English swings by 1.7, because one group's small hours are another group's lunch break. The curve is flat for the same reason a chorus of voices singing at different times sounds like a drone.

Circaseptan

The week barely registers

For all the drama in the hours, the days are almost featureless. The typical edition moves just 13% from its quietest day to its busiest, against up to 1,066% across the hours of a single day. Sunday is the busiest day in 13 of the 23 editions, Friday or Saturday the quietest in 16, and that is very nearly the whole story. Even the most week-shaped edition, Finnish, only spans 39%.

Curiosity, it turns out, does not take weekends off. It only sleeps. And that makes a useful control for the next platform, because GitHub's week is the part that moves.

A second organism

GitHub keeps the calendar Wikipedia ignores

GH Archive publishes every public event on GitHub, one file per hour. Four weeks of it is 104.6 million events and 54.9 GB of compressed JSON, and it records no location at all, so the only clock available is UTC.

That turns out to matter, because it puts GitHub in exactly the position English Wikipedia is in: a population spread around the world, measured on a single clock. Their daily swings come out almost identical. English Wikipedia's day moves by 1.67x; GitHub's human day moves by 1.66x. Neither number says anything about how nocturnal programmers are. Both say the users are everywhere.

Which is the whole argument of this page in one chart. Put all three curves on the UTC clock and German Wikipedia, read almost entirely inside one timezone, swings by 11.5x and comes to a point. The two globally-read services flatten into waves barely a sixth as deep, because at every hour some of their audience is asleep and some is not.

But flat is not the same as identical. The two flat curves are uncorrelated (r = −0.04) and peak six hours apart. English Wikipedia tops out at 20:00 UTC, GitHub at 14:00 UTC, the hour when Europe's afternoon overlaps North America's morning and the largest slice of the working world is at a desk at the same moment. Amplitude tells you how spread out an audience is. The peak tells you whether you are looking at work or at leisure.

The week is where GitHub separates itself. Where the typical Wikipedia edition spans 13% from its quietest day to its busiest, the number of people reviewing code on GitHub falls to 30% of its weekday level on Saturday and Sunday, and stays there.

Method

Counting events would have counted robots

The obvious way to build these charts is to count events per hour. On GitHub that measures the wrong thing, and it says so loudly enough to catch.

Counted as events, GitHub pushes are busier at weekends: 1.04 times the weekday rate. Counted as distinct accounts, 11% fewer people push at weekends than on a weekday. Both numbers come from the same four weeks. The first one is measuring scripts.

In a single sampled hour, the ten busiest accounts produced 24.8% of all pushes, and the heaviest of them carried no [bot] suffix at all. Across the full month, an average of 117 accounts per hour each emitted 50 or more events, between them 15.3% of everything attributed to humans. So the charts above count people, once each, however hard they were typing.

Separating the robots out gives the clearest picture of what a machine's day looks like. Bots as a population rise and fall with the humans who trigger them, because most of them are continuous integration waiting for someone to push. Bot output does something else entirely: it spikes at 01:00 and 12:00 UTC, the hours a scheduled job fires, and it is the same on Sunday as on Wednesday. Its weekend ratio is 1.00.

Longitude

What gets read at three in the morning

This is the question the whole exercise is really for. Wikipedia's hourly API will not answer it, since per-article data stops at daily granularity, so the numbers below come from the raw hourly dumps: all four weeks of them, filtered down to a watchlist of the 4,250 articles that reached the daily top 1,000 at some point in September.

Each article's curve is the median across days of its own daily shape, indexed against the curve of the whole watchlist. Taking the median matters more than it sounds: a first pass without it returned Teachers' Day and a run of US Open players, articles with one enormous day whose single burst set the entire profile. Anything with more than 30% of its views on one day is now dropped outright. 3,100 articles survive.

And the answer, for English Wikipedia, is that the question is broken.

The left-hand column is not a list of nocturnal subjects. It is the Philippines, India, Vietnam, Thailand and Australia. The right-hand column is the United States and Britain. At 09:00 UTC the Americas are asleep and South Asia is wide awake, so an article's hour-of-day profile on a globally read edition is mostly a readout of where its readers live. The clock is measuring longitude.

There is no fix available inside English Wikipedia. There is no timezone to convert to, because the readers do not share one.

The axis that survives

Tuesday is Tuesday everywhere

Day of week has none of the hour's ambiguity. Timezones shift the clock; they do not shift the calendar. Whatever longitude a reader sits at, Saturday arrives for them on Saturday, and the weekday axis is therefore the one place in this data where a difference between two articles is a difference about the articles.

It works. Sorting the same 3,100 articles by weekend index separates them cleanly into sport and entertainment at one end and news, institutions and reference at the other, with no topic labels supplied anywhere in the pipeline.

The cleanest check that the method works is an article that names its own answer. Thursday Night Football sits near the bottom of the weekend ranking with an index of 0.20. Its daily curve reads 0.38, 0.45, 0.50, 1.93, 3.22, 0.25, 0.27 from Monday to Sunday: a spike on Thursday, a bigger one on Friday as people look up what happened, and nothing at the weekend. Nobody told the pipeline when the game is played. It recovered the schedule from timestamps alone.

The same axis picks out working tools without being asked. ChatGPT comes in at 0.69, elevated Tuesday through Thursday and down on both weekend days, which is the shape of something people use at a desk.

One timezone

Asking the question properly

There is one way to get a real answer about three in the morning, and it is to pick an edition where three in the morning exists. Japanese Wikipedia is close to ideal: its readers are overwhelmingly in one country, Japan keeps a single timezone, and it has observed no daylight saving since 1951. Add nine hours to UTC and you have the clock its readers were actually looking at.

So the pipeline was pointed at ja.wikipedia as well: the top 1,000 articles of each sample day, the same four weeks of hourly dumps, the same median-across-days method, the same index against the edition's own baseline. 3,007 articles cleared the filters, across 27 full JST days.

The quiet window immediately moves to where it belongs. On English Wikipedia the six quietest hours were 06:00 to 11:00 UTC, the middle of the night in Chicago and the middle of the morning in Delhi. On Japanese Wikipedia they are 02:00 to 07:00 JST, the middle of the night for essentially every reader.

The afternoon column is not a grab bag. Seventeen of the top twenty-four are sumo: wrestlers, a commentator, the list of tournament champions and the article on the prize banners carried around the ring. The September grand tournament ran from the 14th to the 28th, inside the window. The top division fights late in the afternoon and the broadcast finishes around six, and fourteen of those seventeen peak at 17:00 or 18:00 JST, eleven of them at 18:00 exactly: the hour people reach for a phone to look up the wrestler they have just watched.

Nothing in the pipeline knows what sumo is, when the tournament was held, or which of 3,007 article titles are wrestlers. It has hourly counts and nothing else, and it reconstructed a broadcast schedule from them. That is the same trick as Thursday Night Football in the previous section, run on a different continent against a different clock.

The small hours are the other half of the answer, and they are blunt. Reading down the thirty articles most over-represented between 2am and 7am, the great majority are adult-industry subjects, studios, platforms and performers, with late-night anime and idol groups making up most of the rest. It is the strongest single topical signal anywhere in this project, and it is invisible on English Wikipedia, where 3am is not a time of day so much as a region of the world.

Which is the whole point. Japanese Wikipedia in the afternoon is a second screen for the television. At four in the morning it is something else. The only thing standing between English Wikipedia and the same answer is that its readers do not share a clock.

Colophon

Where the numbers come from

Wikimedia Pageviews API, hourly aggregate endpoint, human traffic only, 23 language editions, 1 to 28 September 2025. 11.7 billion pageviews. Hourly granularity exists only on the aggregate endpoint; the per-article endpoint stops at daily, which is why the topic section uses the dumps instead.

Wikimedia pageview dumps, hourly per-article files, 1 to 28 September 2025, filtered to a watchlist built by unioning the daily top 1,000 across ten spread-out days.

GH Archive, every public GitHub event, 672 hourly files, 1 to 28 September 2025. 104.6 million events, 54.9 GB compressed.

Things worth knowing before quoting any of this

The local-time view is an approximation for five editions. Mapping a language to one timezone is close to exact for Japanese or Polish and rough for English, Spanish, Arabic, Portuguese and Russian. Those five were flagged in the source before the analysis ran, not after seeing which ones came out flat, and they are marked on every chart.

GitHub has no local-time view at all. GH Archive records no location, so every GitHub chart here is on the UTC clock, and its hourly amplitude is damped by exactly the longitude-mixing that flattens English Wikipedia.

Four weeks is four Saturdays. The article-level weekend index rests on four occurrences of each weekday. That is enough to separate rugby from earthquakes, and enough that a single big Saturday cannot carry an article on its own, but not enough to trust a small difference between two individual articles.

The window is September 2025, not this month. GH Archive's recent files are incomplete: a sampled 2026 hour contained 2,248 events and no PushEvents whatsoever, against 165,169 events in the equivalent 2025 hour.

Every chart is scaled to its own average. Nothing here compares absolute traffic between platforms or editions, only the shape of the rise and fall.

Method notes

Each GH Archive file covers exactly one UTC hour, so the filename carries the timestamp and no JSON parsing is needed: type and actor.login sit adjacent in every record, so one regex pass over the decompressed bytes gives the joint distribution of event type and bot status exactly. Checked against a hand-parsed hour, it matched to the event.

The pageview dumps are sorted by domain code, so one edition's block is contiguous and the reader stops as soon as the code no longer matches. That reads 49% of each file for English and 73% for Japanese instead of all of it.

Processes rather than threads throughout: gzip decompression releases the GIL but the regex scan does not, so threads capped out at 1.4x no matter how many cores were available.
